Topic 1.3 · Product hypothesis

Track A · designer

Introduction to the meaning context and the goals of the hypothesis

What we do

  • translate statistics into architectural form by searching for meanings;
  • analyze hidden narratives to position the property in the commercial real estate market;
  • justify capital investment through the property’s identity;
  • avoid drawing floor plans without analysis for the sake of a sustainable economy.

Example: a water tower as a conceptual water museum, not a generic office (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: searching for a meaning context affects the profitability and viability of the concept.
TermsCAPEX — capital expenditure: one-time investment in creating or reconstructing the property.
In plain terms: investment in construction and renovation.OPEX — operating expenses: regular payments for maintaining the property.
In plain terms: monthly building operating bills.
Historical meanings

Why architects should look for historical meanings

Analysis tasks

  • create added value through the history and memory of the place;
  • protect the property from competition with new generic buildings and shopping centers;
  • preserve the facade appearance and the scale of the development;
  • avoid literal quotation of styles and imitation with cheap materials.

Example: a pre-revolutionary tram depot stripped back to brick, with references to the rails (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: historical meanings form the conceptual core for a contemporary architectural language.
TermsAGO — architectural and urban planning appearance.
In plain terms: how the building looks from outside and fits into the street.Urban fabric morphology — the form and structure of the built environment: density, height, and street grid.
In plain terms: the physical framework of the district, its scale and rhythm.
Toolkit

A brief adaptation of text analytics

What we do

  • analyze large sets of historical documents with AI tools;
  • use core-toolset models for quick tasks and local context;
  • use extended-toolset models for extensive historical references;
  • focus on managing the context window, not on finding new services.

Toolsets: Alice AI, DeepSeek core toolset; Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini extended toolset.

Conclusion: finding meanings depends on skill in working with the context window of the models you have studied.
TermsLLM — a large language model, such as ChatGPT, DeepSeek, or Alice AI.
In plain terms: an algorithm for analyzing and generating text.Prompt — a text instruction to the model.
In plain terms: your command to an AI tool.
Reliability

Finding reliable meanings through RAG

How it works

  • exclude facts not supported by data by uploading documents to a closed toolset;
  • limit answers to the uploaded documents;
  • get answers with citations to specific paragraphs in the archives;
  • remove the client’s confidential and financial data before analysis.

Toolset: NotebookLM extended toolset.

Example: analysis of scanned work logs from a weaving factory to identify poor lighting in the workshops (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: closed RAG systems analyze local history materials using verified documents.
TermsRAG — a mode in which the model answers from retrieved fragments of uploaded documents.
In plain terms: the AI tool looks for answers only in your texts, ignoring its training data.
Appearance and neighbors

Analyzing appearance and significant neighbors

What we analyze

  • height, rhythm, materials, and facade color;
  • significant neighbors as generators of audience behavior;
  • the link between the building’s appearance, traffic magnets, and function gaps;
  • rejecting facade materials unrelated to the scale of the street.

Example: a dark one-story 19th-century warehouse among glass skyscrapers as a quiet refuge (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: analyzing rhythm, materials, and scale together with attraction points ensures visual integration of functions.
Verification

Hallucination risks and independent verification

Rules

  • manually check generated dates, names, and events;
  • treat AI tools as text-prediction systems, not databases of truth;
  • use three independent sources for a quantitative claim;
  • control confirmation bias when working with AI narratives.

Example: a literary coworking concept derailed by an AI error in a writer’s address (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: AI generates hypotheses; fact-checking remains with the architect.
TermsHallucination — an answer that sounds confident but is not supported by data.
In plain terms: the AI tool gives a plausible but unverified answer.
Prompt

Adapting the six-part prompt for meanings

What we specify

  • the role of an architectural historian to extract identity;
  • the context boundaries of redevelopment;
  • the task of identifying five key historical meanings in the text;
  • constraints on unverified claims and a check of the permitted use.

Example: a residential complex concept rejected on an industrial warehouse site because of regulatory restrictions (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: the request structure yields relevant meanings that do not conflict with the law.
TermsPermitted use (VRI) — the legally permitted use of the land plot.
In plain terms: what the law allows to be built on this site.
Workshop

Step-by-step extraction of meanings on site

Steps

  • use models with deep search to analyze local history archives;
  • load previously verified historical facts into the context window;
  • send the six-part prompt with a ban on internet search;
  • copy the generated meanings only after assessing their relevance.

Toolsets: Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini extended toolset.

Example: extracting the meaning “a territory of hidden artisan production” from archival references (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: skill in managing the context window lets you extract meanings and still leave time for reflection.
Metaphor

Translating meaning into an architectural metaphor

What we do

  • transform the idea into volume, materials, and light;
  • prepare the concept for city review in the AGR format;
  • look for legible solutions without direct associations;
  • avoid literal quotation of historical facts through decoration.

Example: roof geometry for rainwater collection instead of a round fountain (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: moving from text to spatial metaphor creates enduring architecture.
TermsAGR — an architectural and urban planning solution.
In plain terms: an official document with drawings and visualizations.
Algorithm

Four steps for building a metaphor

Steps

  • isolate the root meaning or a short phrase;
  • look for a natural or physical analog;
  • abstract the form down to geometry and proportions;
  • project the abstract graphic onto building drawings in CAD.

Example: the meaning “intersection of transit flows” translated through a river delta into the pattern of the atrium’s load-bearing ramps (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: the four steps produce logical space-planning solutions without decoration.
TermsCAD — computer-aided design software, such as AutoCAD and similar tools.
In plain terms: programs for drawings and 3D models.
Hypothesis

Forming an architectural hypothesis

What we connect

  • form, audience, and historical meaning into a single manifesto;
  • the concept around the property’s maximum commercial value;
  • the formula: form X, for audience Y, grounded in meaning Z;
  • justifying initial investment through identity and demand.

Example: a permeable ground floor for freelancers based on the meaning of an open market square (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: the hypothesis statement connects form, people, and history for presentation to the client.
TermsHBU — Highest and Best Use: a use that is legal, physically possible, financially feasible, and produces the greatest value.
In plain terms: which use will give the property the most value and income without breaking the law.
Scenarios

Behavioral use scenarios

What we describe

  • break the hypothesis into daily operating cycles for different groups;
  • ensure continuous use of floor areas to cover round-the-clock costs;
  • describe the scenario: who, when, why, and how the building operates;
  • avoid single-use space that sits idle outside working hours.

Example: a morning farmers’ market and an evening indie concert venue in an old hangar (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: 2–3 overlapping scenarios increase the project’s operating efficiency and resilience.
Data

Synchronizing the hypothesis with spatial data

What we check

  • the compatibility of the metaphor with pedestrian movement graphs;
  • exclude conflicts between an aesthetic scenario unsupported by data and the district’s demographics;
  • assess traffic intensity and quality before approving functions;
  • adapt the meaning to the rhythm of today’s user.

Example: a quiet post-station concept clashing with heavy courier and taxi traffic (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: a concept is viable when it is synchronized with people’s behavior and supported by spatial data.
TermsPedestrian graph — a simplified network: attraction points (nodes) and routes between them (edges).
In plain terms: the grid of people’s movements.Passing traffic — people walk past; destination traffic — people come specifically to the property.
In plain terms: those passing by and those heading to the property.
Constraints

Metaphor and structural constraints

What we check

  • audit the building’s technical plans before visualization;
  • transform the idea when it meets a grid of massive columns;
  • block incompatible functions because of sanitary zones;
  • prevent financial losses caused by ignoring soil conditions.

Example: a glass cloud project over a garage halted because the concrete foundations were 70% worn (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: the existing structure and urban planning constraints set the frame for a viable metaphor.
TermsCapital construction facility — a permanent building or structure.
In plain terms: a building you cannot move without approval.BTI — the technical inventory bureau: technical passport and floor plans.
In plain terms: the official technical drawings of the building.Sanitary protection zone — a buffer zone with development restrictions.
In plain terms: an area where housing cannot legally be built.

Track B · developer

Block goals and input data

Key points

  • a commercial project relies on verified data, not intuition;
  • the basis is the property data sheet, the regulatory data sheet, and the surrounding-area tables;
  • the result is a product hypothesis, a tenant profile, and scenarios;
  • capital investment is justified by a calculated concept.

Example: building a premium shopping center on the site of a truck depot without collecting audience profiles (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: the hypothesis rests on a complete set of input documents and numbers.
TermsCAPEX — capital expenditure: one-time investment in creating or reconstructing the property.
In plain terms: investment in construction and renovation.OPEX — operating expenses: regular payments for maintaining the property.
In plain terms: monthly building operating bills.

Factor Z (source)

Why the hypothesis is correct and where the data comes from.

What we record

  • the concept as a testable logical statement;
  • a verified data source for factor Z;
  • clarity of the concept for the team and the investor.

Example: a coworking hypothesis tied to municipal statistics and the surrounding-area table (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: a formula with a source lets you test every decision against data.
TermsProduct hypothesis — a testable statement: “use X for audience Y because of factor Z.”

Idea and hypothesis

An idea and a testable hypothesis

The difference

  • a business idea differs from a hypothesis by its link to a user and to evidence;
  • popular formats without local justification create risk;
  • an idea becomes a tool when an audience and a scarcity factor are added;
  • ideas without confirmation are screened out early.

Example: turning the “food hall” idea into a hypothesis with calculations of passing traffic and the service gap (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: a formalized hypothesis is a filter for commercial risk.
Trends

Market trends in development

What we consider

  • trends help forecast demand by the time the property opens;
  • a trend is a macroeconomic hypothesis, not a guarantee of success;
  • copying formats from other cities without local specifics creates risk;
  • a trend passes through the filter of local reality.

Example: converting a building into a dark store on a central pedestrian street, losing foot traffic (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: a trend sets the direction of demand but is tested against the property’s constraints.
TermsMarket trend — a sustained change in market demand or supply.
In plain terms: the direction in which the market is moving.
Finding trends

AI tools for finding market trends

How we search

  • aggregate current real estate market analytics with language models;
  • use deep-search tools for current publications;
  • give a verifiable source for every number and growth percentage;
  • apply the three-source rule to confirm hypotheses.

Toolsets: Alice AI, DeepSeek core toolset; Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini extended toolset.

Example: finding street-retail trends with references to business publications (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: deep search speeds up trend collection but requires manual statistical checks.
TermsLLM — a large language model, such as ChatGPT, DeepSeek, or Alice AI.
In plain terms: an algorithm for analyzing and generating text.Prompt — a text instruction to the model.
In plain terms: your command to an AI tool.Deep Research — a model mode that gathers and compares many sources and produces a report with citations.
In plain terms: search with a review of sources.
Location

Grounding a trend in local data

What we check

  • the trend’s compatibility with the location’s physical and commercial data;
  • the regulatory data sheet and surrounding-area tables narrow the search funnel;
  • the asking rate is the advertised price; the actual rate is the transaction price;
  • trends not supported by population density and the district economy are rejected.

Toolset: CIAN core toolset.

Example: a dark-kitchen hypothesis rejected because of high rent and low audience density (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: local data protects against investment in formats that do not work.
Prompt

The six-part prompt for hypotheses

What we specify

  • a structured request for reassembling the data;
  • the role of an investment analyst and commercial real estate broker;
  • the property parameters and the goal of finding functions;
  • a ban on unverified knowledge and nonexistent numbers.

Example: an art gallery concept that skips the constraints and format block (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: the six-part prompt produces pragmatic hypotheses in table form.
Data

The input data package for analysis

What we upload

  • the quality of hypotheses depends on the cleanliness and completeness of the data;
  • the data sheet and regulatory data sheet record the constraints;
  • a cleaned competitor table, personas, and asking rates;
  • sums and shares are calculated with formulas in spreadsheets before uploading to an AI tool.

Toolsets: 2GIS, CIAN, Excel, Yandex Sheets core toolset.

Example: a kindergarten proposed in a sanitary zone because the data sheet was not uploaded (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: a complete and clean data package is a condition for viable hypotheses.

Verification

Four questions of viability

What we check

  • run an initial stress test of ideas before financial modeling;
  • assess the market’s willingness to pay for the function;
  • check the physical presence of the target audience;
  • check the building’s capacity and compliance with regulations.

Example: a medical center rejected because of insufficient electrical capacity and solar access (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: the four questions preserve capital at the paper-design stage.
TermsPermitted use (VRI) — the legally permitted use of the land plot.

Sources

The three-source rule

How we confirm

  • every hypothesis is confirmed by three independent sources;
  • combine mapping, economic, and statistical data;
  • when the sources agree, the hypothesis becomes a proven fact;
  • do not use sources that reprint each other’s data.

Example: a shortage of quality offices confirmed by map exports, price lists, and statistics (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: consistent application of the rule turns a hypothesis into an investment argument.
Tenant

The profile of the target tenant

What we describe

  • the profile of the business that leases space and generates income;
  • the operating model type and technical requirements for the building;
  • an estimate of the ceiling rate from the table data; sums and shares are calculated with formulas;
  • the source of the tenant’s customers.

Example: a fitness studio requiring reinforced floors and ventilation (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: the tenant profile adjusts the design brief for the designers.
TermsTarget tenant — the type of business or organization that leases space and pays rent.
In plain terms: who will lease the space and pay the rate.
Scenarios

Behavioral scenarios of the hypothesis

What we describe

  • unfold the hypothesis over time to make the space pay;
  • build an operating schedule to cover round-the-clock costs;
  • describe the scenario: who, when, why, and what they pay;
  • create 2–3 overlapping scenarios for morning, day, and evening.

Example: transit coffee in the morning, coworking by day, concerts in the evening (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: detailed scenario work increases economic resilience.
Conflicts

Testing the hypothesis for conflicts

What we check

  • test the hypothesis for conflicts with the analytics;
  • check its ability to close a real function gap;
  • compare the business model with competitors’ rates;
  • reject hypotheses not supported by numbers.

Toolsets: CIAN, Excel core toolset.

Example: a children’s center rejected because it could not pay median rates (teaching example, not C52 data).

Conclusion: comparing against gap and rate figures protects against loss-making decisions.
