Why

Why HBU matters

Key points

  • HBU compares the site “as if vacant” and “as improved”;
  • economics: compare scenarios before investing in the project;
  • risk: screen out prohibited and physically impossible uses;
  • skip this step and the decision may rest on a use that cannot pay for itself.

Example: C52: current use covers creative business, education, food concepts, and events; alternatives: no data, a PZZ extract is needed; BTI plans from 2005 and field measurements from 2016 exist; a current technical passport is needed.

Conclusion: HBU supports a decision: retain, modernize, or demolish.
TermsHBU (Highest and Best Use) — highest and best use.
In plain terms: which use gives the property the most value.Viability — the ability of a use to pay for itself and work in this building and this location.
In plain terms: the use pays off here.
Context

Context: where HBU comes from

Key points

  • the Appraisal Institute definition: a reasonably probable and legal use that is physically possible, supported by demand, financially feasible, and produces the highest value;
  • the sequence of tests: legally permissible, physically possible, financially feasible, maximally productive;
  • the analysis covers the site “as if vacant” and the property “as improved”: retain, modernize, or demolish;
  • in this course we use a working version: 5–7 scenarios and four tests.

Source: Appraisal Institute, The Dictionary of Real Estate Appraisal, 2022.

Example: C52: current use covers creative business, education, food concepts, and events; “warehouse” is the EGRN designation; alternatives and tests: no data, a PZZ extract is needed; BTI plans from 2005 and field measurements from 2016 exist; a current technical passport is needed.

Conclusion: Context sets the frame, but the decision rests on the property’s data.

Tests

The four HBU tests

Key points

  • legally permissible: the use does not conflict with the PZZ and restrictions;
  • physically possible: the structure and services can support the use;
  • financially feasible: income covers CAPEX and OPEX;
  • maximally productive: the use delivers the greatest value among the permitted options.

GBA, GLA, and common areas

Diagram

GBA → common areas → GLA → premises

Key points

  • GBA is the entire area measured to the outer walls;
  • common areas are the floor area that is not leased;
  • GLA is the area that can be leased;
  • premises are the sum within GLA.
Diagram

Example: C52: we take the EGRN area of 1,959.2 m² as the basis; GBA, common areas, and GLA are refined using the 2005 BTI plans and 2016 measurements; a current technical passport is needed.

Conclusion: GBA includes common areas and GLA; income is calculated from GLA.
TermsGBA — gross building area, measured to the outer walls.
In plain terms: the whole area of the building from outside.Common areas — spaces that are not leased to an individual tenant.
In plain terms: shared corridors, lobbies, and restrooms.GLA — gross leasable area that can be rented to tenants.
In plain terms: the area we actually lease.
Why

Why separate GBA and GLA

Key points

  • income is calculated from GLA, not from GBA;
  • common areas are not leased but still take up floor area;
  • the risk: overstating income by using GBA;
  • skip this step and the floor-area balance does not add up.
Diagram

Conclusion: GBA and GLA are different bases: income and floor areas are calculated separately.
