Colleagues, we are moving on to Topic 2.2, which opens the door to deep financial planning for our project. In Stage 2.1 we selected viable scenarios, made a baseline assessment of their operating efficiency, and built a spatial balance. Now we need to turn that conceptual balance into a professional investment model. And the first thing we must do is build its unshakeable frame.

Teaching example, not C52 data

A small fictional building, a former pumping station, for which the “Yoga and Pilates Studio” scenario has been chosen. Before looking for prices, we assemble a clean financial model skeleton: item names only. Income structure: hire of the large hall (hourly), hire of the small hall, sublease for the herbal bar, membership sales. OPEX structure: utilities (water, heating, electricity), cleaning, security, ventilation maintenance, property tax, waste removal. CAPEX structure by phase: Phase 1 (Survey and design) — architectural design, engineering surveys; Phase 2 (Structural system) — removal of old equipment, strengthening of openings; Phase 3 (Engineering systems) — supply and exhaust ventilation, new risers; Phase 4 (Finishes) — acoustic panels, specialist flooring for the halls, shower cubicles; Phase 5 (Landscaping) — entrance lighting; Phase 6 (Approvals) — obtaining permits. There are no amounts here, only a rigid frame into which we will remember to enter the figures once the quotes are collected.

Build the financial model skeleton

What exactly are we doing at this stage, and why is it separated into a critically important block of our work? Our main task today is to build an exhaustive income structure, capital expenditure structure, and operating expense structure. We must organize the logic so that no line item is missed across any phase of the project. All final financial metrics will be calculated solely by the spreadsheet using strict mathematical formulas; the language model only helps us form the structure by suggesting non-obvious or hidden items we might have forgotten. The key rule at this stage: we do not yet enter prices, market rates, or specific amounts into the model. We are building an empty but maximally detailed skeleton.

Let us look at our baseline example, the C52 property. The building area is 1,959.2 m² according to the EGRN. However, if we request precise line-item CAPEX and OPEX data for C52 right now, we will get a single answer with the status “no data: document needed.” Without a full construction design and actual operating contracts, we simply do not have the figures.

Why it matters and the economics

Why do we separate the creation of the structure from the search for prices so obsessively? In development economics, a missing cost item hurts a project far more than an inaccurate or outdated price. If you forgot to include “foundation strengthening” or “waterproofing” in the capital expenditure structure, and it suddenly becomes necessary once the builders are on site, your project can stall because of a large cash gap. Contractors sit idle, time passes, and there is no money in the budget for that work. A missing item inevitably distorts your final NOI (net operating income, that is, rental income minus operating expenses) and breaks the future payback you have already promised the investor. By building the skeleton before the figures appear, you firmly insure yourself against these blind spots, forcing the AI model to produce hundreds of possible cost lines for review.

Context and history

In classic project management and investment analysis, what we now call a “skeleton” is known as a work breakdown structure. Redevelopment market practice shows that inexperienced developers often build a budget at the highest level, in three or four abstract lines: “renovation,” “equipment,” “facade,” “miscellaneous.” However, the professional planning standard requires deep detail for every project phase, down to the level where a specific item can be handed to a specialist contractor for precise costing. Budgets made of general phrases never pass investors' internal risk review, because unexpected costs always hide behind abstractions.

Why the model skeleton

The model skeleton is far from being just a list of words or a formality. In economics, any item missed at the start distorts your final NOI and puts future payback at risk. The main threat in redevelopment is the risk of hidden costs: they appear much later, when construction is already under way, and always fall outside the originally approved budget. Without a rigid, detailed skeleton, important items constantly fall between project phases. For example, the designer sincerely believes the builders have accounted for ventilation, while the builders believe the client is buying the equipment directly. The result of our current analytical step is a maximally detailed structure of all items before any actual amounts appear in it.

Context and history

When building in a green field, you can control every cubic meter of concrete from scratch. When reconstructing an old building, you are always buying “a pig in a poke.” Budget overruns in redevelopment projects often amount to a significant share of the original budget precisely because no exhaustive cost list was assembled at the start with meticulous attention to all the hidden features of the old stock. The more detailed the skeleton before you set sail, the calmer the voyage.

Context: CAPEX, OPEX, and income

To build a working financial model skeleton, you must clearly separate three groups of cash flows. The first group is CAPEX, one-off investment in reconstructing the property before it starts generating stable revenue. The second group is OPEX, regular, recurring costs of running a building that is already in operation. The third group is income, which the property generates from rent and additional services.

The basic logic of the operating part of the model is simple: income minus OPEX gives NOI. Definition: NOI — net operating income: rental income minus operating expenses. In short: rental income minus expenses.

The key rule at this stage: CAPEX is accounted for completely separately from the operating flow. One-off construction and renovation investment is not deducted from a single month's rent; it lives on its own schedule. Full payback and the metrics NPV and IRR are not considered in this topic; see Topic 2.4. Definition: NPV — net present value; IRR — internal rate of return. In short: discounted project metrics.

Why it matters and the economics

Strict separation of these three buckets protects the model from logical distortion. If you mix one-off capital investment with regular expenses, for example by recording roof or riser replacement as monthly operations, the calculated NOI drops sharply. As a result, an operationally strong property looks loss-making on paper, and a promising project is rejected.

The opposite error is no less dangerous: if you hide regular running costs in the construction budget, operating income appears artificially inflated. Separating the flows lets you see separately how much money must be invested to launch the property and how much it can reliably earn after opening.

Context and history

On the commercial real estate market, the value of a building as an investment asset is usually assessed through its sustainable operating flow, that is, through NOI. Capital expenditure, in turn, forms the cost of creating or renewing the asset itself. That is exactly why professional practice never mixes routine maintenance with large-scale reconstruction: without such separation, it is impossible to assess either the efficiency of building management or the real cost of entering the project.

Why it matters and the economics

Splitting CAPEX into six phases solves the problem of managing money over time. Reconstruction is not paid for in a single installment on day one: survey and design are funded first, then the structural system and engineering systems, and only then finishes, landscaping, and final approvals. If you dump all capital expenditure into one common list without phases, you cannot plan the funding schedule correctly.

In money terms this creates two risks. Either the team raises the entire amount in advance and overpays a significant sum for servicing idle funds, or it skips mandatory early stages, so construction stops midway while waiting for approvals or rework of the structural system.

Context and history

A phased budget structure has long been a basic standard in reconstruction project management. Each phase has its own measurable result and a control point after which a decision is made to move to the next stage. When the budget is broken down by phase from the start, it becomes clear not only to the concept author but also to engineers, cost estimators, and project partners.

We cannot create an adequate cost structure out of thin air. What is needed at the outset to form a reliable financial skeleton? First, a clearly defined function and use scenario (the winner from the HBU tests). Second, detailed property documents: precise areas, structural data, engineering, including TP capacity. Prompt for the CAPEX structure

We move on to the first of the three main requests. This prompt is responsible for forming capital expenditure.

Let us examine this prompt in detail, part by part. The first part is the role: “Act as a project manager in the client's team.” This phrase sets the professional lens. We block the AI model's attempts to answer like a construction blogger or an interior designer. A project manager thinks in phases, risks, and documents. The second part is the variables: “Reconstruction of [building: type] for [function].” Here the algorithm links the property's initial data to the function chosen at the HBU step. The third part is the grouping: “grouped by phase....” We strictly list the six phases: survey and design, structural system, engineering systems, finishes, landscaping, approvals. We do not allow the algorithm to invent its own time structure. The fourth part: “Include items that are often omitted for this function.” We exploit the AI model's ability to find non-obvious connections in vast text arrays, bringing specific threats to the surface. The fifth part is three fields per item: “state the unit of measure, what drives its quantity, and which document is needed.” An item without a unit of measure is useless to a cost estimator. The sixth part is the prohibition: “Do not include prices or amounts.” We cut fantasy off at the root. The seventh part is the format: “a table for transfer to Excel.” The answer is immediately ready for work.

Gap register: document → dependency

When the structure of items is assembled, it becomes clear that we have some of the property data and are missing some documents. So that this shortage does not remain a vague marginal note, we bring it into a gap register following the logic “document → dependency.” The register links each missing document to the organization that issues it and to the specific sections of the financial model that depend on it.

The base register table has four key entries. First row: the PZZ extract, issued by the local government. It determines the permitted function and urban planning restrictions, and therefore directly affects the income block and CAPEX. Second row: the up-to-date technical passport, issued by BTI. It fixes GBA, GLA, and the exact composition of premises, on which both income and the scope of work in CAPEX depend.

Third row: technical conditions on capacity, issued by the utility company. Available capacity, CAPEX engineering items, and OPEX operating items depend on them. Fourth row: structural inspection, performed by a design organization. It determines load-bearing capacity and the need for reinforcement, which directly forms CAPEX items. The main point of this slide is simple: every gap has a specific owner and a specific line of the model, so the register turns the “no data” note into a clear plan of requests.

This step directly links working with an AI model to the fundamental principle of development: “Every figure has a documentary source.” If you simply leave the list of items in a text file, you will not be able to manage it. Transferring it to Excel with the added “Source” and “Assumption” columns moves your work from the “idea collection” format into the “investment control” format. This framework subsequently integrates directly with the net operating income (NOI) calculation we discussed in Topic 2.1. If the OPEX structure is transferred carelessly, your NOI will be false. Moreover, this verified framework is the basis for forming the final financial memorandum (Artifact 2), which we will assemble in Topic 2.5. Without it you simply cannot move to the final stage of the project.

Context and history

In investment modeling there is a rule of “data traceability.” Any auditor or bank inspector checking your financial model must be able to click any figure in the summary table and follow the chain of formulas back to the primary document (agreement, commercial proposal, or market report). If a figure “hangs in the air,” that is, it was entered manually without a stated source and assumption, the entire model is deemed invalid. By adding these columns to the framework right now, we are laying the foundation for the institutional quality of our work.

What should you do if, while transferring the structure, you discover that the AI model did ignore the bans and produced ready-made amounts?

A typical failure and what to do

The most typical failure at the stage of working with a cost structure is when a language model, despite the direct ban in the prompt (“Do not include prices or amounts”), adds a column with indicative prices to the table. The model tries to be overly “helpful.” What should you do in this situation? First: immediately delete those prices and amounts from the answer received. Second: if the table is spoiled, send the request back into the chat, emphasizing the ban on figures even more firmly. Third: re-check the remaining text items against your documents. Fourth: leave any calculations exclusively to the spreadsheet at subsequent stages.

Takeaway

the model structures items excellently, but it does not calculate the economics and does not know current local prices.

Why it matters and the economics

Why are prices suggested by the model so dangerous for your project's economics? First, those figures have no verifiable source. The model may have generated an average repair cost from data three years old. Second, you cannot check what exactly is included in that price (whether it includes VAT, delivery, installation). Third, and most importantly, such figures create an illusion of false precision. If you copy that unfounded figure into your budget and show it to the investor, you fix a cost expectation that in reality will be exceeded several times over. False precision at the start guarantees a conflict with the investor at the finish, when the real estimates from contractors reveal the truth.

Context and history

Language models have a built-in context completion mechanism. When they see a request for a cost structure, their algorithmic experience suggests that such structures are usually accompanied by figures. So even with a ban in place, the probability of generating a random figure remains. This is not a fault of a particular service; it is a feature of the architecture of generative intelligence. Our task is not to re-educate the algorithm but to tightly control the output.

Common mistakes

Four typical errors repeat regularly when assembling a financial model skeleton with language models. First error: a missing item; as a result the income, OPEX, or CAPEX structure is incomplete, and some real flows simply fall out of sight. Second error: reconstruction phases applied to OPEX or income, carrying construction logic into places where the logic of regular operation or revenue sources should work.

Third error: prices and amounts appeared in the model's answer despite the direct ban in the prompt. Fourth error: the grouping of items does not match the working spreadsheet into which the data is to be transferred. The main point of this slide: all four errors are caught reliably not by intuition but by systematic reconciliation of grouping and verification of sources before the first formula enters the spreadsheet.

Why it matters and the economics

Let us examine what each of these four errors threatens the project with in money terms. First error: a missing item. If an income item is missing, the model understates the property's potential, and a promising project may be rejected needlessly. If an OPEX item is missing, operating costs turn out to be understated and the calculated NOI artificially inflated throughout the building's operating life. If a CAPEX item is missing, an unforeseen significant sum arises during reconstruction outside the approved budget, creating the threat of a cash gap.

Second error: reconstruction phases applied to OPEX or income. Mixing one-off investment with regular flows destroys the logic of the net operating income calculation. Regular operating costs are confused with capital ones, the operating budget cannot be properly handed over to management, and the assessment of the function's viability is distorted.

Third error: prices and amounts in the model's answer. Such figures have no verifiable source and create false precision. If you rely on them when discussing the budget, the real commercial proposals from contractors and market rates will later show a substantial divergence, which will lead to a revision of the entire project economics.

Fourth error: the grouping of items does not match the working spreadsheet. When transferred, rows land in the wrong sections or are duplicated across groups. As a result, the final formulas either count the same cost twice, understating NOI, or lose some items when summing, overstating the expected financial result.

Context and history

Structural errors existed long before language models appeared. In ordinary project work, analysts often copy old tables from previous properties, forgetting to change the grouping or deleting needed rows along with the old figures. Working with an AI model speeds up text generation but makes such blunders less noticeable at first glance, because the table looks neat. That is exactly why professional checking always starts with reconciling headings, section logic, and the cleanliness of sources.
